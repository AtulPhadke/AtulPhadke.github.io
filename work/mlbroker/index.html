A simulation of the decision loop. The jobs and prices are made up. The rules come from the design.

The problem

GPU time is expensive and getting harder to find. Teams buy it a few different ways, and every one comes with a catch.

A real ML team mixes several of these while scientists keep queuing jobs. Lean one way and you pay for idle GPUs. Lean the other and experiments wait while deadlines get closer.

Ways to get GPU time
TypeWhat you getThe catch
SpotSpare cloud capacity at a heavy discountThe provider can take it back at any time
On-demandPay by the hour, only while it runs, with no interruptionsYou pay full price for that safety
Capacity reservationA block of instances for a week or two, at a discountYou pay for the idle hours too

Cost

Compute is too expensive to leave idle. The goal is GPUs busy all the time.

Scientists’ time

Queuing a job should be easy, and results shouldn’t wait on infra.

Priority

Jobs aren’t equal. Deadlines and this week’s needs decide what goes first.

Preemption

Some jobs can’t miss their deadline, so lower-priority work gets stopped and resumed later.

Distributed training

Big jobs need many GPUs at once, eventually across providers.

Why a fixed formula breaks

Most schedulers start with a rule like this one. Score each job from a few fixed inputs, run the highest score that fits, and wait otherwise. It works on a normal day. Pick what goes wrong:

  1. The idea: a scheduler that reads the company

    Job priority isn’t static, so the scheduler shouldn’t be either. Every cycle goes around this loop.

    1 Data input

    The queue, budgets and quotas, job type weights, live cloud prices and capacity, and what’s already running. Admins add a note for the week in plain English.

  2. 2 Decision

    Claude reads all of it in one pass and proposes a batch of moves. Code checks each one against the spend cap, quotas, all-or-nothing placement, and human pins, and drops any that break a rule. Kueue runs the rest, on cloud or on-prem.

  3. 3 Runtime feedback

    GPU utilization, memory, interruptions, and training progress flow back in. A new job, a price change, a spot interruption, or a finished run starts the next cycle.

How I’m building it

Part 1 was my co-op at Matterworks. Part 2 is what I’m building now, on my own.

Part 1

A priority scheduler on Kueue

Matterworks.ai, 2026

I built a priority-based job scheduling engine on Kubernetes with Kueue. It handled preemption, quota management, and priority-driven deployment across dozens of on-prem GPUs and cloud clusters, so a 10-person ML team could run dozens of GPU jobs a day.

The rules worked. The problem was that the right priority kept changing.
